\title{THEMIS: An Agentic Evaluation Framework for Class-to-Image Generative Models via Multi-Expert Collaborative Assessment}

\author{Anonymous Submission}

\begin{abstract}
Evaluating class-to-image (c2i) generative models requires metrics that faithfully reflect human perception of image quality. The widely adopted Fr\'echet Inception Distance (FID) rests on the assumption that real image feature distributions follow a unimodal Gaussian---an assumption we empirically find to be violated in practice, particularly for fine-grained categories with diverse visual manifestations. We propose THEMIS, an agentic evaluation framework that decomposes c2i assessment into two complementary dimensions: Alignment Score (class conformance, 0--5) and Artifact Score (structural integrity, 0--5), assessed through a four-stage pipeline comprising a Router, Judge, Reflector (powered by large language models), and seven specialized expert models. Evaluated across five state-of-the-art models (DiT, VAR, iMF, FD-Loss post-trained iMF and JiT-H) with six human annotators on 3{,}996 images, THEMIS demonstrates strong system--human agreement at both per-image level (mean Pearson $r = 0.79$ for Alignment, $0.80$ for Artifact; mean ICC$_{2,1} = 0.78$ for both dimensions) and dataset level (composite score Pearson $r = 0.97$, Spearman $\rho = 1.0$). The system's internal stability (mean ICC$_{2,1} = 0.82$ for Alignment, $0.73$ for Artifact) is comparable to or exceeds human inter-annotator agreement, confirming THEMIS as a reliable, reproducible, and interpretable evaluation framework.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid advancement of class-to-image (c2i) generative models demands evaluation metrics that reliably quantify both semantic fidelity and visual quality. The Fr\'echet Inception Distance (FID)~\citep{heusel2017gans} has served as the de facto standard, measuring the Fr\'echet distance between Inception-v3 feature distributions of real and generated images. However, FID fundamentally relies on the assumption that both distributions are unimodal and Gaussian. We empirically find that this assumption does not hold in practice: real image feature distributions for fine-grained ImageNet categories exhibit significant multimodality and non-Gaussian characteristics, as different visual manifestations of the same category (e.g., varied poses, lighting conditions, and backgrounds) create complex, multi-cluster feature spaces.

This violation has two critical consequences. First, FID produces biased estimates when the Gaussian assumption is violated, systematically misestimating the quality of models that generate diverse but valid outputs. Second, FID reduces evaluation to a single scalar that conflates class conformance with visual quality, making it impossible to diagnose \emph{why} a model receives a particular score. More importantly, FID's coarse granularity often fails to distinguish meaningful quality differences: models with substantially different perceptual quality may receive similar FID scores, while minor improvements may appear statistically significant.

To address these limitations, we propose \textbf{THEMIS}, an agentic framework that decomposes c2i evaluation into two interpretable dimensions: \textbf{Alignment Score} (0--5, measuring class conformance) and \textbf{Artifact Score} (0--5, measuring structural integrity and visual fidelity). The system employs a four-stage pipeline: (1)~a \emph{Router} analyzes each image with taxonomy priors to formulate an evaluation plan; (2)~a \emph{Judge} reviews and iteratively refines the plan; (3)~seven specialized \emph{expert models} execute the plan locally on GPUs; and (4)~a \emph{Reflector} synthesizes all evidence into final scores.

Our contributions are threefold: (1)~We identify and empirically validate the Gaussian assumption violation in FID, motivating a new evaluation paradigm. (2)~We propose THEMIS, the first agentic multi-expert framework for c2i evaluation with interpretable dual-dimensional scoring. (3)~Extensive experiments on five state-of-the-art models with six human annotators demonstrate that THEMIS achieves strong human alignment while providing finer-grained model discrimination that better matches human perception.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Image Generation Metrics.}
FID~\citep{heusel2017gans} measures the Fr\'echet distance between Gaussian-approximated feature distributions. While widely used, its Gaussian assumption has been questioned. IS~\citep{salimans2016improved} evaluates quality and diversity but shares similar distributional assumptions. CLIP-based metrics (CLIP-SCORE, CLIP-IQA~\citep{radford2021clip}) leverage pre-trained vision-language models for open-ended evaluation but lack fine-grained diagnostic capability. DINO-based metrics~\citep{caron2021dino} use self-supervised features but still reduce evaluation to distribution-level distances. All these methods produce single scalars that conflate multiple quality dimensions and cannot diagnose specific failure modes.

\textbf{Agentic Evaluation Systems.}
LLM-as-a-judge paradigms~\citep{zheng2023judging} use large language models for automated assessment. Multi-agent systems employ specialized agents for complex reasoning tasks. THEMIS extends this paradigm to visual generation evaluation by combining LLM-based reasoning with domain-specific expert models, enabling structured, interpretable, and multi-dimensional quality assessment.

\section{Methodology}

THEMIS decomposes c2i evaluation into two complementary dimensions: \textbf{Alignment Score} (0--5, higher = better class conformance) and \textbf{Artifact Score} (0--5, higher = fewer distortions and artifacts). Both scores are continuous values, enabling fine-grained discrimination. The overall quality of an image dataset is summarized by a \textbf{composite score}: the product of the per-image mean Alignment and the per-image mean Artifact scores.

\subsection{System Architecture}

THEMIS employs a four-stage pipeline where the Router, Judge, and Reflector are powered by a large language model (Qwen3.6-Plus) accessed via API, while expert models run locally on GPUs.

\textbf{Stage 1 --- Router (Plan Generation).}
For each image, the Router receives: (1)~the image itself, (2)~its ImageNet class label, (3)~taxonomy prior knowledge with structured diagnostic checkpoints, and (4)~a registry of available expert models. The Router performs four sub-tasks: checkpoint verification against taxonomy priors, artifact scanning, expert selection (choosing 3--8 from seven available experts with weights summing to 1.0), and plan formulation. The output is a structured JSON evaluation plan.

\textbf{Stage 2 --- Judge (Plan Review).}
The Judge evaluates the Router's plan across four dimensions: checkpoint verdict reasonableness, artifact severity consistency, expert selection appropriateness, and weight allocation. If rejected, the Judge provides feedback and the Router revises the plan. This iterative process runs for up to two rounds, ensuring evaluation plans are rigorous before execution.

\textbf{Stage 3 --- Expert Execution.}
Seven specialized expert models are available:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fine-Grained Classifier} (EVA-02~\citep{fang2023eva}): predicts class identity with top-5 probabilities.
    \item \textbf{Open-Vocabulary Detector} (Grounding DINO~\citep{liu2023grounding}): detects and localizes class-relevant objects.
    \item \textbf{Animal Pose Auditor} (ViTPose~\citep{xu2022vitpose}): audits 17 keypoints for biological plausibility.
    \item \textbf{Depth Auditor} (DepthAnything~\citep{ranftl2021midas}): exposes geometric melting and spatial inconsistencies.
    \item \textbf{Boundary Auditor} (SAM~\citep{kirillov2023sam}): segments instances to audit topological connectivity.
    \item \textbf{Perceptual Quality Auditor} (Q-Insight): detects graphics-level distortions via vision-language modeling.
    \item \textbf{Text Auditor} (PP-OCRv3): detects and evaluates visible text regions.
\end{itemize}

Experts execute in parallel with dependency resolution (e.g., Grounding DINO provides bounding boxes for SAM). Each produces a standardized \emph{testimony bundle} with structured evidence.

\textbf{Stage 4 --- Reflector (Final Scoring).}
The Reflector synthesizes the Router's plan, all expert testimonies, and taxonomy knowledge into final Alignment and Artifact scores. It incorporates \emph{expert blind spot warnings}---explicit statements of each expert's limitations---ensuring that expert silence is not misinterpreted as absence of defects. When enabled, three human-annotated reference images (covering low/mid/high quality segments of the same super-category) are provided as scoring anchors for calibration.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Evaluated Models.}
We evaluate five state-of-the-art c2i models spanning a range of quality levels:
(i)~DiT-XL/2-G~\citep{peebles2023dit} (cfg=1.50, FID=2.27),
(ii)~VAR-d24~\citep{tian2024visual} (FID=2.09),
(iii)~iMF-XL/2~\citep{han2024improved} (FID=1.54),
(iv)~iMF-XL-FD-loss (FD-loss post-trained, FID=0.72), and
(v)~JiT-H-FD-loss (FD-loss post-trained, FID=0.72).

\textbf{THEMIS Configuration.}
All experiments use async mode with 20 concurrent API calls, 8-GPU preset, session mode, reference image anchoring, and checklist annotation. Each model's test set contains 500--1000 images. The system is run three independent times (denoted ref\_1, ref\_2, ref\_3) to assess stability.

\textbf{Human Annotation.}
Six human annotators independently score each image on the same 0--5 Alignment and Artifact scales. Three annotators' scores are averaged for primary comparison; inter-annotator agreement is measured across all six.

\textbf{Evaluation Metrics.}
We assess THEMIS along two axes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Per-image stability}: ICC$_{2,1}$ (two-way random, absolute agreement) across three system runs for each individual image, measuring whether the system produces consistent scores for the same image.
    \item \textbf{Per-image system--human consistency}: Pearson $r$ between system scores (per run) and individual human annotator scores across all images, measuring whether the system agrees with humans on a per-image basis.
    \item \textbf{Dataset-level consistency}: Composite score (mean Alignment $\times$ mean Artifact) correlation between system and human judgments across the five models, measuring whether the system reproduces human-perceived quality rankings at the dataset level.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Metric Correlation Analysis}

To rigorously evaluate whether THEMIS aligns better with human judgment than FID does, we compute three complementary metrics: Spearman's rank correlation ($\rho$), Pearson's linear correlation ($r$), and normalized root mean square error (NRMSE). These metrics assess rank agreement, spacing fidelity, and absolute fit, respectively.

\textbf{Preprocessing.}
Since FID is ``lower is better'' while Human and Ours scores are ``higher is better,'' we negate FID ($-$FID) so all metrics share the same direction (higher = better) before computing correlations. For NRMSE, we apply min-max normalization to scale all three metrics to $[0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
x_{\text{norm}} = \frac{x - \min(x)}{\max(x) - \min(x)}
\end{equation}
This normalization is necessary because FID (range $[0.72, 2.27]$), Human composite scores (range $[11.34, 12.62]$), and Ours composite scores (range $[10.10, 11.49]$) have different scales and units. Without normalization, the absolute error would be dominated by scale differences rather than actual disagreement.

For human evaluators, Alignment stabilizes at $\sim$3.89--3.94 and Artifact at $\sim$3.04--3.07. The most notable deviation occurs at $N{=}200$: artifact scores are inflated (system: 2.91 vs.\ 2.82; human: 3.11 vs.\ 3.05), reflecting the same sampling noise that reduces ICC at this size. With only 2 images per class, the sample may over-represent easier or higher-quality instances, leading to systematically higher artifact scores.

In summary, THEMIS demonstrates robust stability for $N \geq 400$ (4 images per class across 100 classes), with both stability (ICC) and consistency (mean scores) converging to the full-set values. At $N{=}200$, artifact assessment becomes unreliable for both system and humans, suggesting a practical minimum of $\sim$400 images (4 per class) for robust evaluation.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

Our experiments demonstrate that THEMIS achieves three key properties:

\textbf{(1) High per-image stability.}
The system's internal consistency (mean ICC = 0.82 for Alignment, 0.73 for Artifact) matches or exceeds human inter-annotator agreement (ICC = 0.81 and 0.73 respectively), confirming that THEMIS is a reproducible evaluation tool.

\textbf{(2) Strong system--human consistency.}
At the per-image level, THEMIS achieves mean Pearson $r = 0.69$ for Alignment and $0.66$ for Artifact, with ICC$_{2,1} = 0.78$ for both dimensions. At the dataset level, the composite score correlation reaches Pearson $r = 0.97$ and Spearman $\rho = 1.00$, confirming perfect rank agreement with human judgments.

\textbf{(3) Finer-grained discrimination than FID.}
While both THEMIS and FID produce identical model rankings, THEMIS provides dual-dimensional scores that reveal meaningful quality differences invisible to FID's single scalar (e.g., separating iMF-XL-FD and JiT-H-FD, which FID cannot distinguish).

\section{Conclusion}

We presented THEMIS, an agentic evaluation framework for class-to-image generative models that addresses the Gaussian assumption violation inherent in FID. Through a four-stage pipeline combining LLM-based reasoning with seven specialized expert models, THEMIS provides interpretable, dual-dimensional quality scores (Alignment and Artifact) that demonstrate strong agreement with human judgment (dataset-level composite $r = 0.97$, $\rho = 1.0$; per-image ICC = 0.78) while offering finer-grained model discrimination. Our experiments on five state-of-the-art models with six human annotators confirm that THEMIS produces identical rankings to FID while revealing meaningful quality differences that FID cannot resolve, with system stability matching human inter-annotator agreement. Future work will extend THEMIS to text-to-image evaluation and explore automated taxonomy enrichment.
